<Context>
Before we write a single line of JavaScript, we need to talk about how computers think. Computers are incredibly fast, but they are also incredibly literal. They cannot guess what you mean. You have to tell them exactly what to do, step by step.
</Context>

<Objective>
By the end of this lesson, you will translate a human-readable problem into a logical sequence of discrete steps (pseudocode).
</Objective>

<Concept>
## Computational Thinking
Programming isn't just about typing code; it is about breaking a large problem into tiny, solvable pieces. Every computer program essentially does three things:
1. Takes **Input** (Information given to the computer)
2. Does **Processing** (Changes or makes decisions based on the input)
3. Produces **Output** (The final result)

### Pseudocode
Before writing real code, programmers often write **pseudocode**—plain English steps that outline the logic.

*Problem*: How do you make a cup of tea?
*Pseudocode*:
1. Boil water in kettle.
2. Place tea bag in cup.
3. Pour boiling water into cup.
4. Wait 3 minutes.
5. Remove tea bag.
6. Serve.

If you miss step 1, the tea is ruined. If you swap step 3 and 4, the tea is ruined. The sequence matters!
</Concept>

<InteractiveSandbox type="classification">
Imagine a program that calculates the total cost of a shopping cart. 
Identify which of the following is the **Input**, which is the **Process**, and which is the **Output**:

A. The final price displayed on the screen.
B. Adding the prices of the items together.
C. The list of items the user wants to buy.
</InteractiveSandbox>

<Feedback>
- C is the **Input** (the data we start with).
- B is the **Process** (the math).
- A is the **Output** (the result).
</Feedback>

<IndependentPractice>
Write pseudocode for the process of "Logging into a website". Break it down into at least 4 discrete steps, including a decision based on whether the password is correct or not.
</IndependentPractice>

<Reflection>
Programming languages change all the time. But the ability to take a messy human problem and break it down into a logical sequence of inputs, processes, and outputs is a skill you will use for the rest of your career!
</Reflection>
